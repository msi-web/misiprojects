How I Turn Data Into Something You Can Trust

Anyone can build a chart. The harder part is defining the right question, trusting the data underneath it, and making sure someone else could retrace every step and land where you did. This is how I actually work through that, end to end.

This content reflects my own point of view, shaped by real experience building and validating analysis for real decisions. It is not tied to one specific project, it is how I would approach any analysis, adjusted to whatever the data, the stakeholders, and the situation actually require.

Why This Matters

A chart can be technically correct and still lead someone to the wrong decision, wrong grouping, a metric that does not mean what it looks like it means, a pattern that is really just noise. The gap between "the numbers are accurate" and "the analysis is trustworthy" is where real judgment lives, and it is easy to skip past if you are moving fast.

The Discipline It Takes

Every step in this process takes real time, defining the actual question, tracing the data back to its source, checking two reports that don't quite agree with each other. None of it shows up in the final visual, but skipping any of it is exactly how a chart ends up technically accurate and still wrong.

How the Steps Connect

Each step exists because skipping it causes a specific problem later, skip defining the real question, and you will build something nobody actually needed. Skip checking the data's structure, and the analysis rests on a foundation nobody validated. Skip documenting the process, and the next person (or the next version of you) has to start from zero. None of these steps are complicated on their own. What makes the difference is doing all of them, every time, not just the ones that fit the timeline.

What Has to Be True

1

Define the Goal

Before touching any data, I get clear on what is actually being solved, who it's for, and whether they will use it. Skip this, and the most polished analysis in the world can still miss the point entirely.

2

Locate the Data

I identify where the data actually lives and whether it is something I can pull myself or need from someone else. Just as important: is it recurring and retrievable, so this does not become a one-time favor I can't repeat next month.

3

Structure the Data

I look at how the pieces connect, the keys that tie sources together, and whether they merge into one clean view or need to stay separate. Getting this wrong does not show up right away; it shows up later, as errors that are hard to trace back.

4

Validate Accuracy

I check for missing pieces and conflicts between sources before trusting any of it. An analysis built on unverified data is not wrong because the math is wrong, it is wrong because the input was never checked.

5

Investigate

I look for patterns, outliers, and frequency, and I chase anything that does not look right rather than letting it pass. The interesting finding is usually the thing that almost got explained away.

6

Decide What to Share

Not everything I find belongs in the final story. I filter down to what is actionable and tied to the original goal, then design the narrative around that, not around everything I happened to notice along the way.

7

Document for Repeatability

I document the process well enough that someone else could retrace it and land on the same conclusion, and well enough that I can run it again myself, on fresh data, without starting from zero.

Every analysis lives in different tools, under different constraints, with different stakeholders. What does not change is that these seven things have to be true somewhere in the process, how fast, how formally, and in what order is the conversation I would want to have with your team.
